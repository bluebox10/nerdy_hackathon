WhyWrong knows why. It maps a specific wrong answer to the specific broken rule underneath it — one of 2,587 diagnosed misconceptions — and hands the tutor a root cause instead of a score. In 1047.21 ms, on one CPU core.

The symptom

A wrong answer is evidence. Almost nobody reads it.

A student subtracts two fractions and writes this:

7/12 − 3/12 = 4/24
A generic AI tutor

“That’s not quite right — try again!”

Detects wrongness. Has no idea what happened. The student retries and makes the same error, for the same reason.

WhyWrong
MISCONCEPTION #172
When subtracting fractions, subtracts the numerators and denominators
Hint 1 · a question, never the answer What happens when you try to subtract the numerators and denominators separately?

Detecting wrongness is easy. Naming which wrongness is what decides whether the next ten minutes help the student or waste their time.

Why it is hard

Most of the taxonomy has no training data at all

The misconception taxonomy is long-tailed and the split is brutal: 1,498 of 2,587 misconceptions (58%) never appear in training, so 562 of the 882 held-out queries ask for a label the model has never seen a single example of.

Scaling a general-purpose embedding model does not rescue this. bge-m3 is 17× larger than bge-small and buys almost nothing — 0.153 to 0.160 MAP@25. The problem is not capacity. It is that nobody taught the model this task.

The method

Train big on our own metal. Distil small. Serve on a CPU.

Everything expensive happens once, offline, on hardware we own. What ships is two quantised models totalling 66.6 MB and a matrix multiply.

What the synthetic data actually did

Adding 17,490 generated pairs to 2,647 real ones — a 6.6:1 ratio — did exactly what it was meant to on the long tail, and quietly wrecked the rest. Recall@25 on unseen misconceptions rose, but MAP@25 on the seen slice fell from 0.255 to 0.167: the generated distribution had taken over the contrastive objective. Oversampling the real pairs 6× recovers the seen slice to 0.244 and keeps the long-tail gain (0.676 → 0.710 unseen R@25), for 0.266 MAP@25 overall against 0.246 for real data alone.

That intermediate regression is in the table above rather than dropped from it. The naive mix is the result most people would have shipped.

Next

What I would build next

  • Disambiguation over guessing. When two misconceptions are both genuinely consistent with an answer, the right move is one targeted question, not a coin flip. The failure gallery says this is the largest error class.
  • Close the loop on sequencing. Naming the misconception is half the job; choosing the next question to prove it is cleared is the other half.
  • Beyond mathematics. Nothing in the architecture is maths-specific. It needs a labelled misconception set, not a new model.
